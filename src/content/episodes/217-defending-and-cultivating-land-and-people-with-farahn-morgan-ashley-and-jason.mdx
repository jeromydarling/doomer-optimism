## Summary

Farahn Morgan, a writer for County Highway, joins Ashley and Jason to discuss the financialization of land and extraction. Drawing from her reporting on a proposed lithium mine in Gaston County, North Carolina, Morgan articulates how contemporary extractivism differs from historical mining: the speed is accelerated, the stakes more naked, the sales pitch more cynical. She speaks to the community's grassroots resistance—disparate people united not by ideology but by place, by rootedness, by knowing their neighbors and wanting to protect them. The conversation moves toward what futures are possible: Morgan finds optimism in the persistence of tactile culture, in maker movements, in small acts of care (a neighbor's garden, a man tending bees), in the return of local storytelling, and in people choosing to sell their land to community members rather than investors. The episode is clear-eyed about systemic extractive pressures while grounded in the belief that margins still hold space for cultivation, for community, for the defense of the common good.

What emerges is not a solution to the energy paradox—we all need it, extraction always harms someone—but a conviction that companies and states have a moral obligation to get buy-in from communities, to be good neighbors, and that when they fail to do so, they deserve resistance rooted in love for place and person.
