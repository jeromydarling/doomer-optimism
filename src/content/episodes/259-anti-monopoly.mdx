## Summary

The history of American antitrust law reveals a recurring battle between concentrated economic power and democratic governance. From the Sherman Act of 1890 through the boom years of the 1940s–70s, antitrust enforcement ensured competitive markets where farmers, small businesses, and entrepreneurs could thrive. But a bureaucratic coup in the Reagan era—disguised as the "Consumer Welfare Standard"—effectively killed the law for forty years. Now, unexpectedly, that consensus is shifting. Lina Khan at the FTC and Jonathan Kanter at DOJ have restored the original meaning of antitrust law: not just cheaper widgets, but decentralized power and the freedom of ordinary people to govern their own economic lives. The real work ahead lies outside tech, in agriculture and healthcare, where billionaire-backed capture runs deepest—and where populist energy, properly channeled, might yet rebuild what was broken.
