## Summary

Ashley Colby Fitzgerald, a tree worker and small farmer in the Berkshires, joins Jason to discuss the agricultural history of New England, the actual purpose of farmers' markets, and what it means to build real community. Drawing on John Michael Greer, Wendell Berry, and Gene Logsdon, Ashley traces how topography shapes settlement patterns and economies—and how affluence has severed the ties of mutual dependence that once held communities together. He offers a clear-eyed critique of permaculture as a product of unprecedented abundance, argues that community can't be conjured by proximity alone, and suggests that the real work of preparing for a leaner future is learning to ask for help.

The conversation moves from regional history to the practical failures of local food systems, the limitations of systems thinking divorced from yields, and the importance of human stewardship in a rapidly changing ecological landscape. Throughout, Ashley emphasizes humility, long-term thinking, and the need to move beyond self-sufficiency toward genuine interdependence—not as a romantic ideal, but as the hard, unglamorous work of staying rooted in place.
