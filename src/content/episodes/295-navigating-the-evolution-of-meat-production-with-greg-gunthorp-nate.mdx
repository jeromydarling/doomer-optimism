## Summary

Greg Gunthorp, an Indiana hog farmer and solar grazer, walks through his unlikely journey from a family farm selling commodity pigs at Depression-era prices to building a USDA-licensed on-farm processing operation. What began as desperation in 1998—when consolidation had crushed the independent hog market—became a pivot toward direct relationships with high-end restaurants like Charlie Trotter's in Chicago. That early success masked a harder truth: the wholesale and food-service model has collapsed for small producers as the big firms have built their own premium brands. Gunthorp argues with unflinching clarity that .3% of U.S. hog volume moves through small processors doing under 10,000 animals per year, while 37 plants slaughter 96% of all pigs. The farm-to-table movement woke the giants without displacing them. Rural America, meanwhile, has been hollowed out—not by accident, but by policy choices that favored consolidation, subsidized land appreciation for the winners, and left most farmers with no viable path to independence. The only enterprises left standing are either corporate, government-controlled, or selling direct to individual consumers. That's not resilience. That's enclosure.
