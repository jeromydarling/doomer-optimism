## Summary

Texas is a state caught between three competing visions—rural, suburban, and urban—each wielding power uneasily and none quite comfortable in the marriage. The privatization of Texas land, once managed by neighborly norms and shared understanding, has created a world where rural people feel disconnected from the outdoors they nominally inhabit, and newcomers find themselves as distant from green space as any apartment dweller in New York. This matters because the federal government now looks to Texas as a model for the West: a privatized, extractive, development-friendly landscape. But John Graves, the great Texas writer who lived through this transformation, showed us something more hopeful—that returning to land you didn't grow up on, learning to tend it well, and refusing both nostalgia and despair is work worth doing, even if you never see the full fruit. The baton has passed to a new generation of regenerative farmers, conservation-minded hunters, and place-based writers. Their work may not save rural Texas, but it will save something worth saving.

The conversation moves from Texas's three-way political standoff through the ecological consequences of privatized land—the exotic animal ranches, the weekend ranch culture, the broken social capital that once allowed neighbors to ask permission and receive it—to the life and legacy of John Graves, whose three books (Goodbye to a River, Hardscrabble, From a Limestone Ledge) document both the loss of a world and the possibility of return. Neither guest indulges in despair or false hope; both see clear-eyed work happening now.
