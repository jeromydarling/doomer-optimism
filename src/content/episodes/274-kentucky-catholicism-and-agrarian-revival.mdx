## Summary

Two Kentucky-rooted men—Tom Ruby, a retired Air Force officer who moved to Persimmon Knob to farm and regenerate forest, and Alan Cornett, a native Kentuckian and former Church of Christ minister—discuss what it means to build a life of place, prayer, and practical work in the age of abstraction. Both adult converts to Catholicism, they trace the long intellectual inheritance of agrarianism from Wendell Berry and the Vanderbilt Agrarians back through Chesterton's distributism to Bonaventure—timeless truths that keep reasserting themselves across centuries and traditions. Kentucky emerges as a landscape of possibility: cheap, beautiful, well-watered land where young families might yet live as neighbors, learn their fields slowly, and build real community. Yet the work requires movements, institutions, and frank talk about the gap between romantic hope and the grinding reality of soil, seasons, and failure. The rise of traditional Catholicism among younger converts, they suggest, signals a hunger for intellectual depth, aesthetic beauty, and religious infrastructure that the culture of abstraction cannot provide—and an instinct that you cannot live well, or live faithfully, without place.
