## Summary

This is the third episode in a four-part series featuring voices from Jonathan Rawles' book *Resilient Citizens: The People, Perils, and Politics of Modern Preparedness*. Rawles presents a vision of resilience rooted not in bunker-building survivalism but in what he calls "slow collapse" thinking—the practical work of establishing functional communities, interdependent networks, and generational rootedness even as broader systems fray. He's brutally honest about the structural headwinds: affordability crises that lock out young people from land ownership, cultural breakdown that makes basic outdoor play a police matter, and economic systems that make productive land use financially impossible. Yet he finds genuine hope in the young people he encounters—Zoomers getting married and having children early, moving back home to build extended-family networks, seeking traditional architecture and land practices that actually integrate with place rather than imposing generic development. The conversation turns on a crucial insight: we cannot wait for the nation to heal. We build resilience by establishing norms, third places, and mutual aid networks in our own communities now, creating pockets of vitality and trust even within a fragmented whole.
