## Summary

Food hubs are not an abstract policy solution—they are the hard work of holding together fragile networks of farmers, institutional buyers, and communities. This roundtable conversation between three food hub operators in different stages (the young Harvest Hub in Humboldt County, the eleven-year-old Tractor Food and Farms in western North Carolina, and the nascent effort in southern Oregon) reveals the unglamorous truth: food hubs succeed not because of grant funding or marketing narratives, but because someone is willing to do the cultural work—to know farmers by name, to take calls, to pay fair prices before systems are "sustainable," and to defend that integrity against the inevitable pressure to scale, certify, and corporatize. The conversation threads through the collapse of regional economies (tobacco in Appalachia, cannabis in Oregon), the difference between rural and urban food cultures, and the looming threat of regulatory capture. Yet the optimism here is hard-won and rooted in something deeper than hope: it is the observation that when you actually pay people, feed your community well, and work with what the land and its people can actually offer, systems tend to persist. The real frontier is not technology or certification—it is the choice, made again and again, to remain accountable to place.
