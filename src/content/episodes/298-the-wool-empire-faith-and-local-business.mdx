## Summary

Greg Sello left a military career—ten years serving as a field artillery officer—to build a rooted family life in northwestern Rhode Island. What began as a homesteading experiment after COVID accelerated into something deeper: a vision of community resilience, intergenerational flourishing, and the restoration of local productive capacity through wool. Greg's wool enterprise idea isn't survivalism or escape fantasy. It's rooted in Catholic social teaching—particularly subsidiarity and the principle that stable communities depend on families managing their own affairs, then building outward. His sheep will produce not just meat and milk, but clothing and employment and bonds between neighbors who haven't grazed livestock in generations. On his seven-acre property, he's cultivating the skills and theological grounding to make it work: a fortress chicken coop, nine garden beds using the no-dig method, berry brambles, and now sheep. The larger vision—restoring the wool economy that once cleared these New England forests—requires both local initiative and permission from a political economy that currently resists it. But that's where his optimism holds: if families are allowed to tend their sovereignty first, and build community from there, something beautiful might grow in the margins.
