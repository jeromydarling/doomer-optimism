## Summary

Doomer Optimism convened a live panel to explore the relationship between acknowledging genuine systemic crises and the possibility of meaningful, even joyful action in response. The conversation ranged widely—from the online homesteading movement to the surprising turn toward traditional religion among metamodern thinkers—but kept returning to a single tension: how to hold realistic despair about collapse without surrendering agency or hope. Panelists insisted that pessimism becomes paralyzing only when it prevents you from showing up to the work, and that work—whether farming, building community, practicing ritual, or making sense together—is what actually dissolves the doom. Recovery, not return. Balance, not purity. Keep your wits about you, they said, because orthodoxy hides everywhere.

The gathering itself modeled what the hosts meant: dozens of people across continents, many meeting for the first time, grappling out loud with theology, governance, technology, and land stewardship. Not solving anything. Listening hard. Committing to the practice of sense-making—that qualitative, intuitive, morally grounded work of asking what matters and why. The failure of our systems, one panelist observed, "fixes itself," which may be the closest Doomer Optimism gets to a credo.
