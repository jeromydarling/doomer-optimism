## Summary

Susan Krumdieck, a mechanical engineer and global pioneer of transition engineering, argues that humanity's greatest challenges aren't "problems" with technical solutions—they're wicked problems rooted in systems thinking. Drawing on the history of safety engineering (particularly the steam boiler crisis of the 1800s, where regulatory discipline and scientific integrity ended decades of preventable explosions), Krumdieck challenges the sustainability narrative and proposes instead that we identify what's unsustainable and engineer disciplined transitions away from it. She reframes the conversation from individual green solutions to systemic change: preventing what's preventable through honest assessment of existing power structures, working from detailed knowledge of how systems actually function, and building local resilience and self-reliance. Rather than waiting for top-down expert consensus or miracle technologies, transition engineering emerges organically from communities and practitioners who recognize unacceptable loss and choose to change. Krumdieck and Josh (an environmental engineer working on regenerative agriculture in Appalachia) explore how this looks in practice: the New Zealand dairy farmer who transitioned to organic production and created a new market; the role of professional engineers in writing standards that make destructive practices illegal rather than optional; and the tension between opting out to build alternatives versus penetrating institutions to reshape them from within. The conversation challenges the optimistic belief that better solutions will simply outcompete worse ones, arguing instead that transition engineering requires moral clarity about what's unacceptable, disciplined methodology to engineer change, and the creation of functioning examples that become new standards—not just possibilities.
