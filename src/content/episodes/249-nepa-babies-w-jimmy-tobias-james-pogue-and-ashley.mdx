## Summary

In this wide-ranging conversation on environmental law and policy, host James Pogue welcomes investigative journalist Jimmy Tobias to examine how federal environmental regulation might shift in the Trump era—particularly in light of the Chevron decision's collapse and incoming Interior Secretary Doug Burgum. The discussion centers on the practical mechanics of laws like NEPA and the Endangered Species Act, which have become both powerful conservation tools and flashpoints in broader conflicts over public lands and development. Tobias brings reporting from his work on endangered species, federal grazing programs, and the origins of COVID-19, offering a nuanced view of how agency capture, bureaucratic inertia, and ideological polarization have hollowed out the environmental movement's ability to articulate a regenerative vision. The conversation grapples with an uncomfortable paradox: while NEPA and ESA rarely stop projects outright, they've come to symbolize a regulatory state many feel excludes them from decisions about their own landscapes. The hosts argue that what's missing isn't better enforcement of existing rules, but a forward-looking vision—one that might reconcile conservation with stewardship, wilderness restoration with community agency, and development with ecological integrity.
