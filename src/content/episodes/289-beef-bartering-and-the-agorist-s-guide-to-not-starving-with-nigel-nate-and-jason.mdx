## Summary

Nigel Best is a carpenter, farmer, and woodworker living near the Ozarks who approaches life through the first principles of food security, shelter, and livelihood. Starting from hardship—working farm labor as a boy, serving in the military—he has built a practical philosophy of skilled trade and regenerative cattle grazing on 76 acres. He's skeptical of both romantic narratives about young people's lack of opportunity and the promises of regenerative grazing theory, arguing that real work requires adapting to weather, economics, and land constraints. His experience suggests that usefulness—whether in carpentry or cattle husbandry—matters more than credentials, and that practical education grounded in labor, not ideology, shapes capable adults.

The conversation examines why young people struggle not because opportunity has vanished, but because they've been pushed toward a single credentialed path while practical trades were condescended. Fitzgerald and Best agree that learning to provide food, shelter, and income should be foundational education by eighth grade, earned through real work on real projects—like Fitzgerald's son working construction after their house burned.
